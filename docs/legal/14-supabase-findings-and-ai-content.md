## Part 1 — What the Supabase check actually found

**The children's data is not in the Supabase account this session can reach.**

The connected account has exactly one project, `radlor-site` (region us-west-2, created 29 Aug 2026). Its entire contents:

| Schema | What is in it |
|---|---|
| `public` | One table: `waitlist` — `email`, `age_band`, `grade`, `source`, `created_at`. 1 row. RLS on, 1 policy. |
| `review` | 11 tables — the Radlor Ops review tool: `videos`, `notes`, `reviewers`, `material`, `issues`, `todos`, `subscriptions`, `testing_sessions`, `invite_links`, `profiles`, `video_reviewers`. |
| `auth` | 10 users — the Ops tool's own logins. |
| `storage` | 42 objects. |

**There is no learner table, no child profile, no `lesson_ids` column, no progress or learning-event table, and no `diagnostic_leads` table anywhere in this project.**

So the question "what is actually stored per child" **cannot be answered from here**. The Milo application's database is somewhere this session cannot see — a different Supabase account or organisation, or not yet created. Until someone points at the right project, the factual premise underneath the whole legal set remains unverified. That premise is the single most important input to documents 02, 04, 07, 08 and 11.

**One genuinely good thing this confirms:** the review tool does not sit beside children's data. It is in its own project with its own logins. That was a standing rule and it is holding.

### Two housekeeping items found along the way

Neither is urgent, and neither concerns children's data — but both are cheap:

1. **Six `review` tables have row-level security switched on with no policies** (`invite_links`, `material`, `notes`, `reviewers`, `videos`, `video_reviewers`). That is deny-by-default, so it is safe rather than dangerous — it means these tables are only reachable with the service role key from the server. Worth confirming that is intentional and not an unfinished policy migration.
2. **Leaked-password protection is off** in Supabase Auth. One toggle; it checks new passwords against known breach lists. The Ops tool has real logins, so it is worth turning on.
3. `review.is_admin()` is a `SECURITY DEFINER` function callable by any signed-in user over the REST API. Probably intentional, worth a look.

### What still cannot be answered from any database

**Which third-party scripts run inside a child's session** is a question about the front-end code, not the database. No amount of SQL will answer it. It needs someone to read the application's code and the network requests a child's browser actually makes. Document 08 currently publishes the claim that there are none — that claim is unverified.

## Part 2 — The lesson content is AI-generated. That changes the ownership question.

The Content Contributor Agreement was raised on the assumption that humans were being paid per topic to write the modules. If the content is AI-generated, the risk does not disappear — it **changes shape**.

### What changes

**In the United States, material generated by a machine without sufficient human authorship is not protected by copyright.** The Copyright Office has been consistent on this and the courts have backed it. The practical consequence for Radlor:

- A competitor who copies the AI-generated modules wholesale may not be infringing any copyright, because there may be no copyright to infringe.
- What Radlor *can* protect is the human contribution on top — the selection of topics, the sequencing, the editing, the curriculum structure, the diagrams as arranged — plus everything the Terms of Service forbid contractually, plus the parts of the product that are software.
- The old Terms said all content "is protected by copyright and other laws". That is an overclaim. Document 12 now flags it for the attorney rather than repeating it.

### What does not change

1. **If any human is still being paid per topic** — to write, edit, review, or fact-check — a written agreement is still needed. Their edits are theirs until assigned. The earlier 49-module brief described exactly this arrangement, so the question is live: *is anyone being paid for this work, in any capacity?* If yes, the agreement is still needed.
2. **Where the AI-generated content came from.** The Grade 4–8 topic structure was derived from a curriculum PDF. Whose document that is, and what it permits, has not been checked. Facts and mathematical ideas are not copyrightable, but a particular expression, sequence or presentation can be.
3. **How the content is described in marketing.** This is the sharper risk of the two. If the modules are AI-generated, marketing must not imply they were written by teachers, by experts, or by named humans. That is a straightforward FTC deception question, it is entirely within Radlor's control, and it costs nothing to get right — but it is easy to get wrong when writing launch copy.

### Recommendation

- **Drop** the Content Contributor Agreement as a blocker **only if** nobody is being paid for content work in any form.
- **Keep** it if anyone is — a short, one-page assignment is enough, and it is far cheaper now than after 49 modules are shipped.
- **Add** a one-line rule to the marketing material rule set: never describe the lesson content as human-written unless it is.
- **Check** the provenance of the curriculum PDF.
