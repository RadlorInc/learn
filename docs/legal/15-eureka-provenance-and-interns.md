# Two Live Risks: the Eureka Math² Topic Names, and the Unpaid Interns

**Date:** 2026-09-22
**Not legal advice.** Both of these have real law behind them and both need the attorney's view. This memo is written so the attorney can be asked a precise question instead of a vague one.

---

## Part 1 — "Some Grade 5 Module 1 topic names are from Eureka Math²"

### The good news first

**Short titles and topic names are, on their own, weak subject matter for copyright.** Names, titles and short phrases are generally not protected. Neither are mathematical facts, concepts, methods, or the idea that multiplication comes before division. If what was taken is genuinely *only* a handful of topic names, the copyright exposure is small.

### Where it stops being simple

Three things are worth separating, because they are different bodies of law with different answers.

**1. Copyright in the sequence, not the words.** A curriculum's *selection and arrangement* — which topics, in which order, grouped into which modules — can be protected as a compilation where it reflects original editorial judgement. The more of Eureka's structure that was followed, the more this matters. "A few topic names" is fine. "Their module structure and topic order, renamed" is a different conversation. **Someone needs to establish honestly which of these it is.** That is a factual question about our own files, and it has not been answered.

**2. Trademark — this is the sharper edge.** *Eureka Math* and *Eureka Math²* are brand names belonging to Great Minds. Copyright and trademark are separate: even where copying a name raises no copyright issue, **using that name in a way that suggests association, endorsement or alignment can raise a trademark issue.** Great Minds has historically taken an active stance on its intellectual property.

Practically:
- Do **not** put "Eureka" anywhere in the product, the marketing, the site, or a reel.
- Do **not** say "aligned to Eureka Math²" or "compatible with Eureka" without the attorney clearing the exact wording first.
- Referring to a *state standard* (for example, a Common Core code) is an entirely different and much safer thing than referring to a *publisher's product*.

**3. Which Eureka, and under what licence.** This matters and is easy to get wrong. The original *Eureka Math* was distributed through EngageNY under an open Creative Commons licence with a non-commercial restriction. **Eureka Math² is a later commercial product and should not be assumed to carry the same licence.** Anyone who reasons "Eureka is Creative Commons, so we're fine" may be reasoning from the wrong product — and the non-commercial restriction in the original licence would be a problem for a paid product in any case.

### What to do

1. **Establish the facts.** Open the Grade 5 Module 1 files and write down precisely what came from Eureka Math²: how many topic names, whether the module structure was followed, whether any wording beyond a title was reused. Do this before asking the attorney anything — it is the question they will ask first.
2. **Rename.** Topic names are cheap to rewrite and are not where the product's value lives. Rewriting them in our own words removes most of the issue for close to no cost. Doing it now, at 36 modules, is far cheaper than doing it later.
3. **Keep "Eureka" out of everything customer-facing**, permanently, unless the attorney clears specific wording.
4. **Record provenance going forward.** For each module, note where the topic list came from. This is a five-minute habit that answers every future version of this question.
5. **Ask the attorney** the narrow question: given the facts from step 1, is there copyright exposure in the selection and arrangement, and what may we say about standards alignment without a trademark problem?

## Part 2 — Unpaid interns testing the content for academic credit

This one deserves more attention than it is currently getting. There are three separate issues, and they are independent of each other.

### Issue A — Whether the internship may lawfully be unpaid

For a **for-profit** company in the United States, an unpaid internship is lawful only where the **intern**, not the company, is the primary beneficiary of the relationship. Courts and the Department of Labor weigh a set of factors, including:

- whether the internship is tied to a formal education programme and grants **academic credit** — this is the factor that most helps here;
- whether the training is similar to what would be given in an educational setting;
- whether it accommodates the academic calendar and is limited in duration;
- whether the intern understands there is no promise of a paid job;
- and — the factor that cuts the other way — **whether the intern's work displaces paid employees or primarily benefits the company.**

"Testing whether the content is correct" is productive work that benefits the company. Academic credit and a completion certificate pull in Radlor's favour; the productive-work element pulls against. It is not obviously wrong, and it is not obviously fine. It depends on the details and on where the interns are.

**The state matters too.** Some states — California among the strictest — apply their own tests that are harder to satisfy than the federal one.

**So the first question is one I cannot answer without knowing: where are the interns located, and which institution is granting the credit?** A US intern at a US company, an Indian student working with an Indian company, and an Indian student working with a US-incorporated company are three genuinely different legal situations. Radlor is a Delaware corporation, which makes this worth getting right rather than assuming.

### Issue B — Intellectual property, again

This is the one that is certain rather than uncertain, and it is the same problem raised earlier about paid contributors — **being unpaid does not change it.**

If an intern reviews a module and writes a correction, rewrites a question, or improves an explanation, **that contribution is theirs until it is assigned in writing.** There is no implied assignment because someone was a volunteer; if anything, the absence of payment makes it harder to argue for one. A company that later discovers it does not own parts of its own curriculum, during diligence or a fundraise, has an expensive problem.

**Fix:** a short written agreement, signed before an intern touches anything. One page is enough. It needs to cover:

- assignment of all work product to Radlor;
- a warranty that their contributions are original and not copied from anywhere;
- confidentiality — they are seeing an unreleased product;
- a clear statement that this is an unpaid educational placement for academic credit, that there is no promise of employment, and what the intern receives;
- a rule that they must never access real children's data or real user accounts.

That last point deserves emphasis. **Interns must test against test accounts and seeded data, never against real children's records.** If a real child's data is reachable by an intern, that is a security and COPPA problem that has nothing to do with internship law. This should be verified rather than assumed, in the same way as everything else in this set.

### Issue C — The university's requirements

Institutions granting credit usually have their own paperwork: a placement agreement, a named supervisor, defined learning objectives, and sometimes an evaluation at the end. Some also require the host company to carry insurance. Radlor should ask each institution what it needs rather than wait to be told — a certificate issued outside the institution's process may not count for the student's credit, which would be a poor outcome for people doing work for free.

### What to do

1. **Tell me where the interns are and which institutions are involved.** The analysis genuinely forks here.
2. **Get the one-page agreement signed before any further review work** — this part is cheap, certain, and does not need to wait for the attorney.
3. **Confirm the interns cannot reach real children's data.** Verify it; do not assume it.
4. **Ask each institution for its placement paperwork.**
5. **Ask the attorney** whether the arrangement satisfies the primary-beneficiary test in the relevant jurisdiction, and have them review the one-page agreement.
